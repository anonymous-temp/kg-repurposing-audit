## S7. End-to-end embedding ablation

To relate the present protocol to the earlier one, DistMult was trained end to end on the seed-42 random-edge partition, with the training CtD edges in the graph. It used the v0.2.0 hyper-parameters: 100 dimensions, binary cross-entropy, four corruptions per triple, Adam (learning rate 0.003, weight decay 10⁻⁶), batches of 32,768 and forward and inverse relations. Only the corruption scheme differed between the two runs. Scores of the full evaluation grid were saved every two epochs, and the checkpoint with the highest validation AP was evaluated (Supplementary Figure S3).

Table S7. End-to-end DistMult on the seed-42 random-edge partition.

| Corruption | Selected epoch | Validation AP | Test pooled AP | Test per-disease AP | MRR | Hits@10 | AUROC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| All entities (v0.2.0) | 4 | 0.031 | 0.057 | 0.092 | 0.187 | 0.358 | 0.665 |
| Same type as true tail | 10 | 0.154 | 0.082 | 0.071 | 0.235 | 0.384 | 0.681 |

Type-constrained corruption raised validation AP five-fold and improved test pooled AP, MRR and Hits@10. End-to-end DistMult nevertheless stayed well below the graph head on the same partition (0.082 vs 0.249 in pooled AP; Section S4). With all-entity corruption, validation AP was highest at the first saved checkpoint and declined afterwards. The end-to-end model optimises the treatment relation jointly with 23 other relations, and with all-entity corruption only about 0.3% of its treatment corruptions are diseases. The graph head, in contrast, is fitted only to the treatment labels on top of fixed embeddings. The comparison is an ablation of the earlier recipe, not a tuned benchmark of end-to-end models.

## S15. Computation and use of AI tools

All computations ran on a shared 4-core CPU server without a GPU. For Hetionet, embedding pretraining took 30 minutes; the hyper-parameter selection about 20 minutes; the write-back experiment about 95 minutes per task and 20 minutes for E2; the bootstrap about 10 minutes per set; the two role-restricted policies another 20 minutes. For PrimeKG, embedding pretraining took about 40 minutes for 10 epochs, the selection 12 minutes and the write-back experiment about 20 minutes per task and 15 minutes for E2. Retrieval of the ClinicalTrials.gov arm records took about 5 minutes. For releases v0.5.0 and v0.5.1, the model-free selection analyses (Section S20) took about 5 minutes per graph, the placebo and testing-intensity experiments about 60 minutes per task in Hetionet and about 35 to 50 minutes per task in PrimeKG, and their bootstraps about 5 minutes per set. An AI system (Anthropic Claude, Claude Opus 5.5, used through Claude Code) assisted with the analysis code for releases v0.3.0 to v0.5.1, the retrieval of trial arm records, the drug-role rules and the check of 50 role assignments (Section S16), both codings of the 80 case dossiers against the codebook and their adjudication against registry records, posted results and publications (Section S21), the figures (matplotlib) and the drafting and editing of the manuscript and this supplement. The second coding was made without access to the first. An earlier version of the code and text (release v0.2.0) was produced with OpenAI Codex. No generative image model was used. The authors reviewed the outputs and are responsible for the content.
